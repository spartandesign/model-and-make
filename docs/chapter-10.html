Working Drawing of the Deck

Turn your model into a drawing someone could build from · work solo. A model lives on your screen. A working drawing communicates it to everyone else — a machinist, a teammate, or your future self — with enough information to build and measure the part without ever opening the 3D file. Making one is a core manufacturing skill, and it’s where the dimensioning you learned by hand in Week 2 becomes a real CAD drawing.

Read the transcript

A model lives on your screen. A working drawing lets anyone build and measure your deck, without opening the 3D file. In Onshape, start a new drawing from your deck model. Place the top view and the front view. Onshape makes them from your model. Set the scale to one to one, and the units to millimeters. Now dimension it: the overall length and width, the thickness, and the kicktail height. Add the hole spacing and the hole diameter. Note the hole as the bolt size plus your clearance. Same rule as Week 2: each size only once. Fill in the title block: the part name, your name and the date, the scale, the units, the material, and a drawing number with a revision letter. Then trade drawings with a classmate. Could they build your deck from the drawing alone? If something's missing, that's exactly what you fix.

Monday — review the conventions

Quick refresher from Week 2 — the rules are the same on paper or in CAD.

  • Orthographic views. Top, front, and right, laid out in third-angle projection (top above front, right to the right of front).
  • Dimensioning rules. Use extension and dimension lines; dimension each feature once, clearly; show overall sizes and feature sizes (holes, thickness).
  • The title block. The drawing’s label — part name, who drew it, scale, units, material, and a drawing number.

Block 1 — create the drawing and place views

  • Start a drawing. In Onshape, from your fingerboard deck model, create a new Drawing.
  • Place the standard views. Insert the top, front, and right views — Onshape generates them from your model; your job is to arrange them in third-angle layout.
  • Set scale and units. Choose a sheet scale (1:1 works for a fingerboard) and confirm units are millimeters.

Block 2 — dimension, title block, export

  • Dimension it. Add overall length (96 mm), width (32 mm), thickness, kicktail height, the mounting-hole diameter (note it as bolt + clearance), and the hole spacing.
  • Fill the title block. Part name, your name, date, scale, units (mm), material (PLA), drawing number, revision.
  • Add notes if needed, then export PDF. Your drawing should now read like a complete working drawing: views, dimensions, and title block.
  • Peer check. Trade drawings with a classmate — could they build your deck from the drawing alone? If anything’s missing, that’s what to fix.

Try it: is this drawing complete?

Pick the dimensions a working drawing of the deck needs. Then press Check my drawing. You'll find out what's missing and what doesn't belong.

Title block — what each field is for

FieldWhat it tells the reader
Part nameWhat the part is (Fingerboard Deck)

ScaleDrawing size vs. real size (1:1 = same size)
UnitsHow to read every number (mm)
MaterialWhat it’s made of (PLA)
Drawing # + RevHow to track the drawing and its version

The standard for a good working drawing is completeness: every dimension needed to make and measure the part is there, stated once, clearly, with no guessing. The “could a classmate build it from this?” test is exactly how a real shop judges a drawing — and in Week 19 each capstone group makes a section and assembly drawing of their park modules, so the bar you set here carries forward.
