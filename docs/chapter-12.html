Fingerboard Rack

Slot-together plywood, sized with your kerf number · work solo or in pairs. You’ll design a slot-together plywood rack that holds your fingerboards — and it’s where your Week 12 kerf number does real work. The panels join with tab-and-slot joints that press together with no glue, so the slot width has to account for the strip the laser removes. Measure, design to fit, and your teacher cuts it in plywood.

Monday — measure and recall your kerf

  • Measure the plywood thickness. Caliper the plywood your teacher will cut (about 3 mm) — the tab-and-slot joints are sized from this.
  • Measure the assembled board. Caliper a finished fingerboard’s width and height (with trucks and wheels) so the board slots hold it without forcing.
  • Recall your kerf. Pull your Week 12 kerf value — slots cut a little wider than drawn, so you’ll subtract the kerf for a snug press fit.

Block 1 — design the rack

  • Lay out the panels. Design a simple rack — a base and uprights (or two side panels and a spine) that hold two or three boards. Keep every panel within the laser bed and the plywood sheet.
  • Add the joints with kerf compensation. Where panels cross, draw tab-and-slot joints. Make each slot = plywood thickness − kerf so the tab press-fits snugly without glue.
  • Size the board slots. Make the slots that hold the fingerboards a little wider than the assembled board so each board drops in and stands without forcing.
  • Engrave (optional). Add a name or logo on a panel face.
  • Submit. Your teacher cuts and engraves your rack in plywood.

Block 2 — assemble and evaluate

  • Press-fit the joints. Fit the tabs into the slots. Snug and square with no glue is the goal — that’s your kerf compensation working.
  • Load your boards. Stand your fingerboards in the rack. Do they sit without forcing and without falling over?
  • Evaluate and log. Were the joints too tight, too loose, or just right? Record the result and one change.

This is your Week 12 kerf number made real — a press fit that holds with no glue. It’s the same skill the capstone needs: park sections that snap to one connection standard, sized from a measured kerf and clearance. Too tight won’t go together; too loose falls apart.
