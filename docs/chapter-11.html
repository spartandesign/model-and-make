How a laser cutter works

  • A focused beam removes material. The laser burns a thin line along your design. Because the beam has width, it removes a strip — so cuts aren’t exactly on the line. That strip is the kerf.
  • Cut, score, engrave. High power cuts all the way through; low power scores a fold line on the surface; a back-and-forth pass engraves a shaded image. Same machine, different power and speed.
  • Vector vs. raster. The laser follows your lines to cut and score (vector), and sweeps across an area like a printer to engrave (raster).
  • Focus matters. The beam has to be focused at the material surface to cut cleanly — out of focus, it won’t cut through.

Our two lasers — and when to use each

  • Dremel LC40 (CO₂). A 40-watt CO₂ laser. Cuts and engraves wood, acrylic in any color, leather, paper, and cardstock — the main cutter for the course’s wood and cardboard work.
  • Flux Ador (20 W diode). A diode laser for finer detail. Cuts wood, plywood, leather, card, and dark or black acrylic. It can’t cut clear or light-colored acrylic. Ours is the standard model, without the color-printing module.
  • Rule of thumb. Clear or colored acrylic → the LC40. Wood, leather, card, or dark material → either machine. Thicker parts or faster cuts → the LC40.

Material settings

Every material needs its own power and speed. Too much power scorches or over-cuts; too little doesn’t cut through. Your teacher tunes settings per material and will show you a material-test grid — small swatches cut at different power/speed values — so you can see which settings cut cleanly and engrave crisply.

Safety (laser)

  • Your teacher runs the lasers this week. From Week 13, licensed students run their own jobs, always with the teacher in the room.
  • Fume extraction runs the whole time, and the lid stays closed while cutting.
  • Approved materials only — never PVC, vinyl, or unknown plastics; they release toxic gas.
  • Laser plus wood or paper is a fire risk — the machine is never left running unattended.

Measure the kerf

This is your hands-on part. Your teacher cuts a coupon of a known size (for example, a 20.0 mm square and a comb of slots); you caliper the result and find the kerf.

  • Predict first. Before measuring, write down whether the cut-out square will come out bigger or smaller than 20.0 mm, and by how much.
  • Measure the coupon. Caliper the cut-out square. Drawn size − measured size = the kerf, the width the beam removed.
  • Record settings and kerf. Fill the card below from the demo: material, which machine, the best cut/engrave settings your teacher used, and your measured kerf.
  • Work out the compensation. To press-fit a tab into a slot, the slot must be narrower than the tab by the kerf. Note that adjustment — you’ll use it on the Week 13 rack.

Kerf and clearance are the same idea on two machines. A real tool never makes exactly what you draw — the printer lands a little off (your Week 5 deviation) and the laser removes a strip (your kerf). The skill is the same: measure the difference, then design around it. You now have that number for both machines — exactly what next week’s rack and the capstone connection standard need.
