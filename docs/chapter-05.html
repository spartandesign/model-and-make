Height is often a little short, because the first layer gets squished into the bed. No printer is perfect, and that's okay. A hole the exact size of its peg won't fit. Make the hole a little bigger, and it fits. That extra room is called clearance, and your measured deviation helps you choose it.

Block 1 — Model and submit your cube

  • New part. Create a new Onshape document named “Calibration Cube – [Your Name]” and confirm units are inches.
  • Sketch the base. On the Top plane, sketch a square and dimension it 2.000 in × 2.000 in.
  • Extrude. Extrude the square 2.000 in to make a cube.
  • Verify before you submit. Use the Measure tool to check all three dimensions read 2.000 in — this is your “designed” number.
  • Export and submit. Export the part as a print file (STL or 3MF) and submit it with your name in the filename.

Between sessions, your teacher runs the print queue. Once you hold your 3D Printer license, you can slice and start your own prints with your teacher in the room. Submit by the end of Tuesday’s block. Your teacher prints on Wednesday, so your cube will be waiting on Thursday.

Block 2 — Measure and log

  • Measure three axes. With calipers, measure the cube’s width (X), depth (Y), and height (Z) to 0.001 in. Measure across the flat faces, not the rounded edges.
  • Fill the accuracy log. Record each measured value and compute the deviation (measured − designed).
  • Compare axes. Which axis is off the most? Height (Z) often differs from width and depth — note what you find.

What you’re seeing: accuracy vs. tolerance

Your cube is close to 2.000 in but almost certainly not exact — no machine is perfect. A few reasons why:

  • Material shrinkage — plastic shrinks slightly as it cools, pulling dimensions in.
  • First-layer squish (“elephant’s foot”) — the bottom layer is pressed into the bed, so the base can read a touch wide and the height a touch short.
  • Nozzle and flow — tiny differences in how much plastic comes out add up over a 2-inch part.

The big idea — tolerance: accuracy is how close your part came to the number you designed; tolerance is the range of “close enough” a part can be and still work. In the capstone, 12 groups’ park sections must snap to one connection standard — too tight = interference, too loose = slop. Knowing your printer’s typical deviation, measured here, is how you pick a tolerance that fits.

What to submit

  • A photo of your printed cube.
  • Your completed accuracy log (all three axes plus deviations).
  • One sentence — which axis deviated most, and your best guess why.
  • One sentence — if you had to design a hole for a 2.000 in peg on this printer, would you make it exactly 2.000 in? Why or why not?
